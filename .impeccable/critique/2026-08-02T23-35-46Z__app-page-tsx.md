## Design Specificity Verdict

**Authored, not category-interchangeable.** Four things could only belong to this product: the wordmark split at its own morpheme boundary (morph + the -eme of phoneme); the eight-hue concept ring keyed to a linguistics taxonomy; warm paper with a serif editorial voice against a geometric sans logotype; and "Hints cost XP" stated as a named principle rather than a feature. Swap the copy for another product and none of it survives.

The one structurally generic block is "How it works": eyebrow label, three columns, title plus paragraph each. That is the default marketing module. It is executed with restraint, but it is the only part of the page a competitor could lift unchanged.

**Deterministic scan**: 7 findings in `app/page.tsx`, all one rule, all advisory: `design-system-font-size` at lines 14, 32, 39, 46, 57 and two more, values 2.75rem / 0.88rem / 0.85rem / 0.8rem. This is the documented 13-step type drift already raised with the user and awaiting their decision. Not a false positive, but not new either. Zero findings in every other category.

**Browser evidence**: contrast measured on every text node against its effective background — **zero failures**. Heading order clean (h1 → h2 → h2). One `main`, one `footer`, no images without alt. Page does not scroll sideways at 390px.

## Overall Impression

The page looks like a real product made by someone with a point of view, which is rare and hard-won. What it does not do is sell. It explains the problem NACLO has, explains what the site adds, and asks you to start — but it never once shows you a problem. For a product whose entire value is the experience of working a puzzle with graded hints, the landing page is a page of prose about puzzles.

Biggest opportunity: put a real problem on the page.

## What's Working

- **The wordmark carries meaning.** Colouring `-eme` at the true morpheme boundary makes the logo teach the concept the product is named for. Most logos decorate; this one argues.
- **Honest, checkable numbers.** "30 problems · 48 languages" beside the CTA, and a footer that says outright the site is unofficial and needs no account. For a teenager deciding whether to trust an unknown study site, that does more than any reassurance copy.
- **The colour system reads as an index, not decoration.** Every hue sits beside the word it encodes, so the code is learnable rather than something to memorise.

## Priority Issues

**[P1] The page never shows a problem**
- *Why it matters*: The product's whole claim is that a transcribed problem with a hint ladder beats a PDF. A visitor has to take that entirely on faith and click through to find out. This is the single largest gap between what the product is good at and what the page communicates.
- *Fix*: Put one real specimen above the CTA — a short data table from an existing problem with one blank in it and the hint control visible. Static is fine. The Ho-Chunk stress table or the Paiwan progressive table would both fit in five rows.
- *Suggested command*: `/impeccable shape`

**[P1] On a phone the primary action is below the fold**
- *Why it matters*: Measured: CTA top at 896px against an 844px viewport — 1.06 screens. Every mobile visitor must scroll past two paragraphs and three explainer columns before they can act. Students study at a computer per PRODUCT.md, but they *discover* on a phone.
- *Fix*: Cut the second paragraph to one sentence and move the CTA above "How it works". The explainer is reassurance; it belongs after the ask, not in front of it.
- *Suggested command*: `/impeccable layout`

**[P2] "written with AI and human-reviewed" is in the second sentence**
- *Why it matters*: This is a process disclosure sold as a headline. For the stated audience — 12 to 17, often on their first problem, deciding whether this site is trustworthy — leading with "AI" invites exactly the doubt the sentence is trying to settle. The human review is a genuine guarantee and it is buried inside a clause about tagging.
- *Fix*: Remove it from the opening. State it as a promise where it reassures: near the CTA or in the footer, phrased as "Every problem is checked against NACLO's official solution by a person before it goes up."
- *Suggested command*: `/impeccable clarify`

**[P2] The page ends on a wall of zeros**
- *Why it matters*: The last thing before the footer is eight rows reading 0/18, 0/14, 0/7 with empty bars. By the peak-end rule the final impression is a list of everything the visitor has not done, on a page they have not yet acted on. "What is in here" is doing double duty as a library summary and a progress tracker, and for a first-timer only the first meaning applies.
- *Fix*: For a visitor with no progress, drop the counters and bars and show the eight concepts as a plain coloured list — the library's range, not a scoreboard. Swap to coverage only once something is solved. The component already knows: it computes `anySolved`.
- *Suggested command*: `/impeccable onboard`

**[P3] Brand name capitalised against its own rule**
- *Why it matters*: "Morpheme tags every problem" breaks the lower-case commitment recorded in PRODUCT.md, in the paragraph that introduces the product.
- *Fix*: Lower-case it, or recast the sentence so it does not open a clause.
- *Suggested command*: `/impeccable clarify`

## Persona Red Flags

**Jordan (First-Timer, 14, sent a link by a teacher)**: Reads two paragraphs before learning anything concrete about what solving looks like. Hits "morphology 0/18" and cannot tell whether that is a lot, whether it is hard, or where a beginner starts. Clicks "Start practicing" and lands on 30 problems sorted by year with no "start here". Nothing on the home page has told them what an easy problem looks like.

**Sam (Curious Puzzler, has never heard of NACLO)**: PRODUCT.md names this audience explicitly and confirms the site cannot lean on contest framing. The lead sentence is "prepare for the North American Computational Linguistics Olympiad" — contest framing in the first line. This visitor does not want to prepare for anything. They want a puzzle. They have no reason to keep reading.

**Alex (Returning Student)**: Best served of the three. The coverage block is genuinely for them. But they land on the same page as everyone else, scroll past the whole pitch every visit, and their actual entry point — the library — is one generic button away.

## Minor Observations

- "Pick a concept" is a 19px-tall inline link, below the 24px WCAG 2.5.8 target. It sits in a sentence, which is the documented exception, so this passes — noting it only so it is a decision rather than an oversight.
- "Computational Linguistics" appears twice in the opening sentence.
- The `-eme` colouring is invisible to a colour-blind visitor and to anyone reading the tab title. It is delight rather than information, so it costs nothing, but it does mean the mark's idea only lands in full colour.
- No `nav` landmark. Correct for a page with one link, worth revisiting when a second destination appears.

## Questions to Consider

- What if the hero were a problem instead of a sentence about problems? The strongest thing this product owns is the moment a table has a blank in it and a hint button beside it.
- Who is this page actually for? It currently addresses the contest-prep student in its first line and the casual puzzler nowhere, though PRODUCT.md commits to both.
- If a visitor read only the first screen on a phone, what would they know? Right now: the name, and that NACLO publishes PDFs.
- Does "What is in here" want to be a scoreboard on someone's first visit, or a menu?
